About woometer

woometer is a small hobby site that grew out of friendly family conversations about what we each believe. It lists hundreds of popular claims, from astrology and homeopathy to ghosts and cover-ups, and lets you answer each one honestly. Most of us already reject most woo. This is just a fun way to see where you draw the line.

What counts as woo?

Woo is a belief that people build their lives around, even though it can't be checked, or has been checked and keeps coming up short.

  • Some woo can never be tested. Is there a spirit world? Did you live a past life? No experiment could ever settle questions like these, so they stay a matter of faith forever.
  • Some woo has been tested, again and again. Astrology and homeopathy have both been put to careful, fair tests, and they do no better than chance.1, 2 People still believe in them anyway.
  • Woo has a following. There are teachers, books, products and communities built around it, and people shape their health, money or choices by it.

What doesn't count: a rumor about a particular person, however wild, isn't woo. It's a question about one person's life, and records and evidence can settle it. Everyday sayings and odd bits of trivia that nobody builds a life around are left out too.

How it works

  • Yes puts a claim in your Believe It list, No puts it in the Trash Bin, and Don't Know sets it aside.
  • Your woo score is the share of your Yes and No answers that were Yes. Don't Know answers don't count either way.
  • Your cat changes as your score does. Tap a claim in your Believe It list to read the evidence, and send your link to a friend to compare.

Why do these beliefs go together?

If your Believe It list has a few health claims, a ghost or two and a cover-up story, you're in good company. Studies keep finding that these beliefs travel together: people who believe one conspiracy theory tend to believe others,3, 4 and belief in the paranormal tends to come along too.5

That isn't because believers aren't smart. These ideas speak to things every human brain wants. Here are some of the reasons they tend to arrive as a bundle.

  • When trust breaks, it breaks widely. If a doctor once brushed you off, or an official turned out to be lying, it makes sense to start wondering who else isn't telling the whole story. A general feeling that "the authorities cover things up" helps explain why people often hold several conspiracy beliefs at once, even ones that contradict each other.4 Alternative remedies and cover-up stories both step into that gap, and "they're hiding the cure" joins them together.
  • Our brains are quick to find patterns. We all see meaning and intention in coincidences sometimes. People who are more likely to see patterns in random noise are also more likely to believe in both conspiracies and the supernatural.6 Synchronicities, omens and "nothing happens by accident" all reward that same instinct.
  • Secret knowledge feels special. Being one of the few who sees what's really going on is a powerful, good feeling. People with a stronger wish to feel unique are more drawn to conspiracy explanations.7
  • Big events feel like they need big causes. It's deeply unsatisfying that a tragedy could come from a small, random cause, so a grand explanation can feel more right than the boring one.8
  • A clear story gives back a sense of control. Illness, loss and uncertain times are frightening. People turn to conspiracy explanations more when they feel anxious, uncertain or powerless.9 A plan for staying well, or an explanation of who's behind it all, can make the world feel a little more manageable.
  • Belonging matters. We all want to feel good about ourselves and the groups we belong to, and these beliefs can serve that need too.9 They often come with warm, welcoming communities, and once your friends, your favorite podcasts and your feed all share one outlook, the next idea they share arrives already trusted.
  • Wellness and conspiracy have grown together online. Natural health and spiritual communities have blended with conspiracy content so much that sociologists coined a word for it: "conspirituality."10

If someone you love believes some of this

Everyone has a few beliefs that don't hold up, and most of us came by them for very human reasons: we trusted someone, we were scared, or it simply made sense at the time. Gentle questions and real curiosity tend to go much further than facts thrown like rocks. woometer is meant to be a friendly place to have that conversation, not to keep score against anyone.

Taking care of yourself

Holding unusual beliefs is not a mental illness, and most people who hold them are perfectly well. But beliefs like these sometimes grow out of anxiety or a feeling of powerlessness, and research suggests they don't usually ease those feelings and may even add to them.9

If beliefs are bringing you a lot of fear, making you feel watched or unsafe, pulling you away from people you love, or straining your family, it's worth talking to a doctor, psychologist or counselor. The same goes if you're worried about someone close to you. Asking for help is a strong, sensible thing to do.

  • In a crisis or feeling overwhelmed: call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US). It's free, confidential and open 24/7, and it's for any kind of emotional distress, not only thoughts of suicide. You can also chat online.
  • To find mental health support near you: call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357. It's free, confidential, open 24/7, and helps individuals and families in English and Spanish.
  • Outside the US, your doctor or local health service can point you to support.
