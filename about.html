About Woometer

Woometer is a small hobby site that grew out of friendly family conversations about what we each believe. It lists hundreds of popular claims, from astrology and homeopathy to ghosts and cover-ups, and lets you answer each one honestly. Most of us already reject most woo. This is just a fun way to see where you draw the line.

How it works

  • Yes puts a claim in your Woo Pile, No puts it in the Trash Bin, and Don't Know sets it aside.
  • Your woo score is the share of your Yes and No answers that were Yes. Don't Know answers don't count either way.
  • Your cat changes as your score does. Tap a claim in your Woo Pile to read the evidence, and share your link to compare with friends.

Why do these beliefs go together?

If your Woo Pile has a few health claims, a ghost or two and a cover-up story, you're in good company. Researchers have found again and again that these beliefs travel together: someone who believes one conspiracy theory is much more likely to believe others, and belief in the paranormal tends to come along too.

That isn't because believers aren't smart. These ideas speak to things every human brain wants. Here are some of the reasons they tend to arrive as a bundle.

  • When trust breaks, it breaks widely. If a doctor once brushed you off, or an official turned out to be lying, it makes sense to start wondering who else isn't telling the whole story. Alternative remedies and cover-up stories both step into that gap, and "they're hiding the cure" joins them together.
  • Our brains are built to find patterns. Noticing the rustle in the grass kept our ancestors alive, so we're wired to see meaning and intention even in coincidences. Synchronicities, omens and "nothing happens by accident" all reward that same instinct.
  • Secret knowledge feels special. Being one of the few who sees what's really going on is a powerful, good feeling. It gives a sense of purpose that's hard to find elsewhere.
  • Big events feel like they need big causes. It's deeply unsatisfying that a tragedy or a pandemic could come from a small, random cause, so a grand explanation can feel more right than the boring one.
  • A clear story gives back a sense of control. Illness, loss and uncertain times are frightening. A plan for staying well, or an explanation of who's behind it all, makes the world feel a little more manageable.
  • Belonging matters. These beliefs often come with warm, welcoming communities. Once your friends, your favorite podcasts and your feed all share one outlook, the next idea they share arrives already trusted.
  • Wellness and conspiracy have grown together online. Yoga, natural health and spiritual communities have blended with conspiracy content so much that sociologists coined a word for it: "conspirituality."

If someone you love believes some of this

Everyone has a few beliefs that don't hold up, and most of us came by them for very human reasons: we trusted someone, we were scared, or it simply made sense at the time. Gentle questions and real curiosity tend to go much further than facts thrown like rocks. Woometer is meant to be a friendly place to have that conversation, not to keep score against anyone.

Further reading

  • Goertzel (1994), Belief in conspiracy theories, Political Psychology. Belief in one conspiracy predicted belief in others.
  • Wood, Douglas & Sutton (2012), Dead and alive, Social Psychological and Personality Science. People can even endorse conspiracies that contradict each other.
  • Darwin, Neave & Holmes (2011), Belief in conspiracy theories: the role of paranormal belief, Personality and Individual Differences.
  • van Prooijen, Douglas & De Inocencio (2018), Connecting the dots, European Journal of Social Psychology. Seeing patterns in noise goes with both conspiracy and supernatural belief.
  • Douglas, Sutton & Cichocka (2017), The psychology of conspiracy theories, Current Directions in Psychological Science. The needs for understanding, safety and belonging.
  • Ward & Voas (2011), The emergence of conspirituality, Journal of Contemporary Religion.
