About woometer

woometer grew out of conversations about what we each believe. Answer hundreds of popular claims honestly and see where you draw the line.

What counts as woo?

Woo is a belief people build their lives around, with teachers, books and communities behind it, that either can't be tested (is there a spirit world?) or has been tested and keeps failing. Astrology and homeopathy, for example, do no better than chance.1, 2 A rumor about one particular person isn't woo, since evidence can settle it, and neither are everyday sayings or trivia.

How it works

  • Yes puts a claim in your Believe It list, No in the Trash Bin, and Don't Know sets it aside.
  • Your woo score is the share of your Yes and No answers that were Yes. Don't Know answers don't count.
  • Tap a claim in your Believe It list to read the evidence.
  • Share your link with a friend to compare your score and all your answers with theirs.

Why do these beliefs go together?

People who believe one conspiracy theory tend to believe others,3 and paranormal beliefs often come along too.4 That isn't about intelligence. These ideas meet needs every human has:

  • Lost trust spreads. Once you feel the authorities cover things up, many stories start to fit, even ones that contradict each other.5
  • We look for patterns. People who see patterns in random noise are more drawn to both conspiracies and the supernatural.6
  • Secret knowledge feels special. A wish to feel unique draws people to hidden explanations.7
  • Big events seem to need big causes. A random, small cause can feel too unsatisfying to accept.8
  • Wellness and conspiracy have blended online, so much that sociologists coined a word for it: "conspirituality."9
  • Stories feel like control and belonging. These beliefs grow when people feel anxious or powerless, and they help people feel good about themselves and their group.10

Everyone holds a few beliefs that don't hold up, for very human reasons. Gentle questions go much further than facts thrown like rocks.

Taking care of yourself

Holding unusual beliefs is not a mental illness. But these beliefs can grow out of anxiety, and they rarely ease it.10 If beliefs bring a lot of fear, make you feel watched, or pull you away from people you love, talking to a doctor, psychologist or counselor can help. The same goes if you're worried about someone close to you.

  • In a crisis: call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US). It's free, confidential, 24/7 and for any emotional distress. You can also chat online.
  • To find support: the SAMHSA National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357, is free, confidential and 24/7. Outside the US, your doctor can point you to help.
